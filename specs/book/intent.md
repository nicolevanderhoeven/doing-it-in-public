# Book Intent

*Written by Nicole van der Hoeven | Updated April 5, 2026*
*This supersedes the April 5 draft intent — Nicole's own words from the writing session.*

---

## In Nicole's words

> AI has changed knowledge work forever. The proliferation and mass availability of LLMs has made it so that AI can not only think for us, but be creative for us. It can write books and poems, sing songs, create videos, paint images, and write code. But there's that old tweet I like that said something like "A future where robots paint and write poetry while humans do menial tasks is not the future I signed up for." The question of whether AI CAN do our knowledge work for us is beside the point. The point is: do we want it to?
>
> At the same time, boycotting AI entirely seems extreme. I'm not one to turn down an extremely potent tool. I would rather learn not just how to use it, but also how not to be used BY it. I want to explore what it means to use AI to supplement my thinking, not supplant it. I want to use AI to supercharge my own learning and my own joy.
>
> Learning in public has always been my favourite way to learn, even before AI. But now I think it's even MORE important than ever. Learning in public holds us accountable to the process of learning when it's just so tempting to let AI worry about the output. But having the output doesn't mean we have the knowledge. The real knowledge worker, a real lover of learning, needs to employ substantial discipline to keep doing the thinking and the creating — not just as a way to safeguard a career, but just for the sheer pleasure of learning. In a world where AI can produce till the cows come home, showing the PROCESS of learning, not just the output, is what will distinguish learners from producers. In this book, I want to demonstrate how to Show Your Work even (and especially when) you use AI.
>
> I also want to talk about learning in public in terms of observability. I think there's a lot that we can draw on from the observability of computer systems, especially when humans and AI are collaborating. How can we make things transparent and visible? How can we default to the garage door being up, so that your real brain work, and not just AI output, is traceable and visible?
>
> I still believe everything I said in the original introduction and contents of the book, but I'd like to add the above thoughts and make it so that working with AI is a central question in the book.

---

## Distilled thesis

AI can do your knowledge work for you. The question isn't whether it can — it's whether you want it to.

Learning in public is the discipline that keeps you doing the actual thinking and creating: not just to protect your career, but for the sheer pleasure of learning. In a world where AI can produce endlessly, showing the *process* of learning — not just the output — is what distinguishes learners from producers.

**In one sentence:** Use AI to supercharge your learning; use learning in public to make sure it's still yours.

---

## The central tension

AI offers the output without the learning. Learning in public refuses that bargain — and demands you show your work, especially when AI could have done it faster.

---

## Essential features (non-negotiable)

1. **The AI question** — central, not peripheral. Every chapter touches it.
2. **Observability** — the systems engineering lens. Transparency, traceability, the garage door. Nicole's signature.
3. **The non-hustle frame** — learning in public for the joy of learning, not to build an audience or protect a career (though those are fine side effects).
4. **"Show Your Work with AI"** — the practical mission. Concrete, honest, lived.

---

## What this book is NOT

- Not anti-AI
- Not a how-to guide for specific tools (tools change; principles don't)
- Not a hustle-culture book about building your brand
- Not a boycott or a warning — an invitation

---

## Who this is for

Anyone doing knowledge work who is navigating the AI age and wondering what "authentic" contribution looks like when AI can generate everything. Not exclusively tech workers — writers, researchers, educators, creators — anyone who makes things from thinking.
