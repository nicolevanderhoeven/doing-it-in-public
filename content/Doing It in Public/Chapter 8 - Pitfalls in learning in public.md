Learning in public is not without its hazards. The same openness that makes it powerful also creates specific ways that it can go wrong — not dramatically, but gradually, in ways that are easy to miss until you're already in trouble. This chapter is about the pitfalls: not to scare you off, but to help you navigate around them, or recognize them early enough to course correct.

## Optimal quitting and exit criteria

We're terrible at quitting. Western culture in particular treats persistence as an unambiguous virtue and quitting as a character flaw. "Winners never quit and quitters never win" is treated as self-evident wisdom rather than the motivational non-sequitur it actually is. The problem is that knowing when to stop is one of the most valuable skills you can develop, and the stigma around quitting makes it very hard to exercise that skill clearly.

Annie Duke, in her book *Quit*, argues that the best time to set your exit criteria is before you start. When you're still excited about a project, still optimistic about its potential, still before the sunk cost fallacy kicks in — that's when you should decide what conditions would make you stop. "If I haven't gained X new readers in six months," or "if this project stops being fun for two consecutive months," or "if I'm consistently dreading doing this rather than enjoying it." Written down in advance, these criteria can save you from persisting past the point where the cost exceeds the benefit.

### Art vs exhibitionism

There's a meaningful distinction between sharing your work because you think it might be useful or interesting to someone else, and sharing your work because you want to be seen. Both motivations are human and neither is entirely wrong, but getting them confused causes problems.

Exhibitionism — the compulsive sharing of everything without filter or purpose — is not the same as learning in public. It looks similar from the outside, but it comes from a different place and tends toward different outcomes. When the primary motivation is being seen rather than sharing something of value, the feedback loop you're optimizing for is attention rather than learning. That distinction matters more than it might seem.

## When is feedback noise? When you should stop caring what people think.

Feedback is essential to learning in public. It's also, often, noise. And distinguishing between the two is one of the hardest skills to develop, because it requires a kind of calibrated confidence — enough confidence in your own judgment to know when to ignore feedback, paired with enough humility to genuinely consider it.

Not all feedback is equally worth considering. Feedback from people who have thought carefully about what you've made, who have relevant experience, and who are engaged in good faith is worth a great deal. Feedback from people who are reacting to a thumbnail, a title, or the first sentence of an introduction — without having engaged with the substance — is worth considerably less. And feedback from people who are just unkind, who are projecting, or who are confused about what you were trying to do, is worth essentially nothing.

### The comment section and its discontents

The comment section is one of the great paradoxes of public learning: it's where feedback lives, but it's also where some of the least useful responses aggregate. Developing a healthy relationship with comments means reading them without being enslaved to them, engaging with the constructive ones without getting drawn into arguments with the unconstructive ones, and knowing when to close the tab.

## Oversharing and maintaining privacy (personal and professional)

Learning in public has a gradient, and at one end of that gradient is oversharing. Oversharing erodes trust — of the people whose information you're sharing without full consent, of the employers who might not want their proprietary work discussed publicly, of the family members and friends who didn't sign up to be characters in your public narrative. The fact that you feel comfortable sharing something doesn't mean everyone else involved has consented to having it shared.

Professional oversharing is its own category. There's a meaningful difference between sharing what you're learning — which is almost always fine — and sharing what you're working on — which requires more care. Proprietary processes, client details, unreleased products, strategic decisions — these are things where the appropriate response is usually to share the learning without sharing the specifics. The pattern "I learned X while working on something I can't talk about" is more useful than it sounds.

### One for you, one for them

One framing that's helped me think about the public/private balance is this: for every piece of content I make that's primarily for my audience (what they want to see, what gets engagement), I try to make something that's primarily for me (what I want to explore, what I find genuinely interesting regardless of whether it performs). This "one for you, one for them" balance keeps the work honest and keeps me from becoming a content machine that has lost the thread of what it's actually trying to learn.
