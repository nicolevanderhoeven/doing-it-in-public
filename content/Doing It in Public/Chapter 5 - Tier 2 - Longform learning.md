At some point, you've built enough of a foundation — notes, habits, a consistent practice of showing up — that you're ready to go deeper. Longform learning is what happens when you take all that microlearning and start synthesizing it into something more substantial: blog posts that actually explore an idea, videos that take thirty minutes instead of three, projects that span weeks or months instead of days. This is where the compounding starts to become visible. This is also where it gets harder.

## The value of failed experiments

Here is something counterintuitive: some of your most valuable public work will be the things that didn't work. Not because failure is inherently educational (it can be, but it can also just be failure), but because documenting failure publicly is genuinely useful to other people in a way that success stories rarely are.

When something works, it's tempting to present it as if it was always going to work — as if the path was clear and the result was inevitable. But when something fails, you have no choice but to be honest about what happened, why you thought it would work, and what actually went wrong. That honesty is rare. It's also extremely useful to someone who is currently considering trying the thing you tried and failed at.

I've written post-mortems on tools I adopted and abandoned. I've published videos about workflows that seemed promising and turned out to be more trouble than they were worth. These consistently get some of the most engaged, most grateful responses I receive. People appreciate being spared a detour.

### Leaning into your unfair advantage

Everyone has an unfair advantage — a combination of experience, background, perspective, and knowledge that is genuinely unique to them. The problem is that we're often blind to our own. The things that come most naturally to us are the things we most underestimate, because ease of access makes them feel ordinary rather than valuable.

## Creating high-effort content based on signals

Once you've been learning in public for a while, you start accumulating signals: topics that consistently get engagement, questions that keep appearing in your DMs, gaps in the public record of a subject that you're uniquely positioned to fill. High-effort content — the kind that takes real time and thought to produce — is most worth making when it's responding to genuine signal rather than following a hunch.

The signal-based approach to content creation is more disciplined than it sounds. It means resisting the urge to make the thing you find most interesting and instead making the thing that most needs to exist. Sometimes those are the same thing. But when they're not, following the signal is usually the better choice.

### Repurposing content to broaden your reach

A good idea doesn't have to live in only one form. A talk becomes a blog post becomes a newsletter issue becomes a series of social media posts becomes a chapter in a book. Each format reaches a different audience. Each repurposing requires some adaptation — you can't just dump a transcript and call it a post — but the core ideas can travel across formats with less effort than creating entirely new content each time.

## What if no one's watching?

This is a question that every public learner faces, usually in the first six months: you're posting, consistently, thoughtfully, and... nothing. No comments. No shares. No responses. The silence can feel worse than negative feedback, because at least negative feedback suggests someone read it.

The honest answer is: a lot of the time, no one is watching. That's fine, and here's why — the benefits of learning in public don't require an audience. Writing clearly, for a hypothetical reader, forces you to understand things well enough to explain them. Building a public record of your learning creates a compounding asset even if no one accesses it yet. The habit of consistent practice doesn't require applause to be worth maintaining.

But also: audiences build slowly, then suddenly. The overnight success that took five years of posting into the void is one of the most common stories among people who eventually built meaningful communities around their work.

### Batching, exploring overlaps between your interests

Batching is the practice of doing similar work together to take advantage of being in the same mental mode. If you're going to write three blog posts this month, it's more efficient to write them all in the same week, in the same sessions, when your brain is in "writing mode," than to context-switch in and out of writing across four weeks. The same applies to recording, editing, researching — any task with a significant cognitive startup cost benefits from batching.

Exploring overlaps between your interests is a more creative strategy: it's about finding the places where two or more of your interests intersect, and making content that only someone with your specific combination of interests could make. The Venn diagram of "observability engineering" and "personal knowledge management" is not large, but I am perfectly positioned to live in it.
