This book has been largely optimistic. I've made the case, chapter by chapter, for the benefits of learning in public: faster feedback, more accountability, stronger communities, compounding knowledge, a more sustainable practice of creation. But I want to end differently. I want to end by talking about what happens when you don't do it — because the costs of not learning in public are just as real as the benefits of doing it, and they're often less visible until it's too late.

## Burnout and overwork

Burnout is frequently described as a problem of doing too much, but the actual mechanism is more subtle than that. People burn out not just from overwork, but from doing work that doesn't have visible progress, doesn't receive feedback, doesn't feel meaningful, and doesn't connect to other people. Private learning, especially private learning in a professional context where you're required to produce visible output for someone else, can have all of these properties.

When your learning is invisible, your growth is invisible. When your growth is invisible, it's easy to feel like you're not growing, even when you are. This disconnection between effort and perceived progress is one of the most reliable paths to burnout I know.

### Cognitive dissonance

There's a specific form of cognitive dissonance that affects knowledge workers who are constantly learning but never sharing: the gap between what they know and what they can demonstrate. This gap creates a nagging sense of fraudulence that has nothing to do with actual competence. The person who has learned something deeply but kept it private often struggles to claim that knowledge publicly, because they have no public record of having done the work.

Learning in public generates its own receipt. Every post, every note, every question asked and answered in public is evidence that you've been here, thinking about this, working on it. The absence of that record is a real loss — not just for potential audiences, but for yourself.

### The Starving Artist mentality

The Starving Artist is a cultural archetype: the brilliant creator who suffers for their work, who remains obscure, who is eventually discovered and appreciated — but only after death, or disaster, or some other reversal of fortune. This archetype is romantic and destructive in equal measure. It normalizes the idea that serious creative work requires obscurity and financial insecurity, and that making your work visible and economically viable somehow compromises the work itself.

Learning in public is a direct challenge to the Starving Artist mentality. It says: you don't have to wait to be discovered. You can participate in the process of making your work findable. You can build an audience, develop a community, and create economic value from your learning without compromising its integrity.

## Business and financial cost: having nothing to show for your work

Knowledge workers produce value by learning things and applying what they know. But if the evidence of that value is locked inside your head, or inside a private notebook, or inside work you did for clients under NDA — you have an asset that you own but can't access. Your track record is invisible. Your expertise is unverifiable. Your reputation is nonexistent outside the narrow circles that have directly worked with you.

This is not just a philosophical problem. It has concrete professional and financial consequences. The person with a public body of work — a portfolio of writing, a GitHub of contributions, a history of talks, a website that documents their thinking — is more findable, more hireable, more likely to be invited to interesting projects, and more able to charge appropriately for their work than the person who has done equivalent work in private.

## Getting it wrong late: training the monkey first

There's a productivity concept sometimes called "eating the frog" — tackling the hardest thing first, before you've run out of energy or willpower. There's a related concept from project management that I find even more useful: training the monkey before building the pedestal.

The phrase comes from a useful thought experiment: if your project requires both building an elaborate pedestal and training a monkey to sit on it, which do you do first? Most people build the pedestal — because it's concrete, plannable, achievable. But the monkey is the risk. If you build the pedestal first and then discover the monkey can't be trained, you've wasted the time you spent on the pedestal. If you train the monkey first, you discover early whether the whole enterprise is viable.

In learning in public, "training the monkey" means testing your ideas, your voice, your content, your audience connection — early, cheaply, publicly — before you've invested heavily in infrastructure and polish. The people who wait until everything is perfect before sharing anything are building pedestals before they've trained the monkey. By the time they're ready to share, they've invested so much that the cost of changing course feels unacceptable.

## An invitation

I started this book with a story about learning Dutch — a language I had no business learning, in public, before I was ready, in front of an audience that was kind enough to tell me when I was wrong. I'm ending it with the same invitation I gave myself at the start of that adventure:

What if you did it in public?

Not perfectly. Not with the right equipment, the right audience, the right level of expertise. Not when you're ready, because you will never be ready. But now, with what you have, from where you are.

The worst thing that happens is that nobody watches. The best thing that happens is that you become someone who learned something, made something, shared something, and helped someone you'll never meet become a little bit less lost than they were before they found your work.

That is enough. That has always been enough.

Go do it in public.
