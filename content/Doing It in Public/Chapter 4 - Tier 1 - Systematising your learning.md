At some point, the daily TIL and the scattered social media posts start to accumulate into something that could be more than the sum of its parts — if only you had a system to make that happen. Systematising your learning means building the infrastructure that turns individual notes into a body of knowledge, and individual acts of sharing into a coherent public presence. It doesn't have to be complicated. But it does have to be intentional.

## Turning notes into a Personal Knowledge Management system

A PKM system is what happens when your notes start talking to each other. In a simple collection of notes, each note is an island. In a PKM system, notes are linked to each other in ways that surface unexpected connections, create emergent insights, and make it easier to find what you need when you need it. The difference between a folder of notes and a PKM system is the difference between a pile of index cards and a library.

I use Obsidian for this, with a vault that now contains over 13,000 notes. I want to say upfront: you don't need 13,000 notes. You don't need Obsidian. You don't need anything fancy. What you do need is some kind of second brain — a place where your thinking lives outside your head, where it can be organized, connected, retrieved, and built upon.

## Creating your own site

Publishing your notes to the public internet is a meaningful step up from posting on social media. A personal site is a home base — a place you control, that won't change its algorithm, won't shut down your account, and won't change its terms of service in ways that make your content inaccessible. Everything you post on someone else's platform is, in a sense, borrowed.

### Version control and gitting gud

Git was designed for code, but it's a remarkably good tool for any kind of writing that benefits from version history, public collaboration, and transparent change management. When you put your notes or your writing on GitHub, you get a public record of every change you've made, when you made it, and why. This is the ultimate public changelog — and it doubles as proof of work.

This book itself lives in a public GitHub repository. Every draft, every edit, every chapter that got cut is recorded. That's not just transparency for its own sake; it's also a way of showing that the messy middle of making something is real, and documented, and nothing to be embarrassed about.

## How to keep some things private and some things public

Not everything should be public. This is obvious — but the line between what should and shouldn't be shared is less obvious than it sounds, and getting it wrong in either direction has real costs. Oversharing erodes the trust of the people whose information you're handling. Undersharing keeps you from the benefits that learning in public provides.

Information segmentation and regulation is the practice of deliberately deciding what lives where. Some notes are clearly private: medical information, personal reflections, client details, things said to you in confidence. Some notes are clearly public: technical learnings, book summaries, process documentation. The interesting and useful space is everything in between — and having a system for thinking about it is better than making ad hoc decisions every time you want to share something.

## How to organise your notes: namespaces, filenames

The naming conventions you choose for your notes will either make them findable or make them a graveyard of information you know exists but can't locate. Good naming conventions are consistent, descriptive, and date-aware. They make it possible to search your notes and find what you're looking for without having to remember exactly how you phrased the title when you wrote it.

### The value of a public changelog

A public changelog is a running record of what you've changed in your public notes and why. It's borrowed directly from software development, where changelogs are standard practice for communicating what changed in a new version of an application. For a public learner, a changelog serves a different purpose: it shows that your thinking evolves, that you update your views when you encounter new evidence, and that your published notes are a living record rather than a fixed artifact.

## Learning in public when you have attention deficit challenges

ADHD and other attention-related conditions are overrepresented in the community of people who are drawn to learning in public — and there are good reasons for that. Learning in public provides external structure when internal structure is hard to maintain. The accountability of a public commitment can replace the executive function required to self-motivate. The variety and novelty of constantly learning new things provides the stimulation that keeps an ADHD brain engaged.

But learning in public also presents specific challenges for people with attention deficit challenges: hyperfocus on new projects followed by crash-and-burn disengagement, difficulty maintaining consistent publishing schedules, the tendency to start new threads before finishing old ones.
