Every epic journey starts with someone deciding to take a step before they're sure they know where they're going. Microlearning is that first step — the lowest-friction, lowest-commitment, highest-accessibility entry point into learning in public. It's the tier that anyone can start today, with tools they already have, without needing a system, a website, or a plan. This chapter is about how to start before you're ready, what to share when you don't know what you're doing yet, and where to share it.

## Building a foundation for learning in public

The hardest part of learning in public isn't the "public" part — it's the starting. Most people have an idea for something they want to share, then spend six months preparing to share it, then either share something overproduced and exhausting, or don't share anything at all. Microlearning short-circuits this by making the bar so low that there's genuinely no excuse not to clear it.

### Starting before you're ready

You will never be ready. This is not a motivational platitude — it's a structural fact about learning. By definition, you don't know what you need to know to do the thing, because if you already knew it, you wouldn't need to learn it. Starting before you're ready isn't reckless; it's the only logical option. The alternative — waiting until you're ready — is just waiting indefinitely, dressed up as preparation.

I had zero Dutch when I decided to learn Dutch. I had zero load testing experience when I wrote my first k6 script publicly. I had never written a book when I started this one. The pattern here is not that I got lucky; it's that I started before I felt qualified, and then the doing qualified me.

### What if it could be easy?

We've been trained to equate difficulty with value. If something was easy, it probably wasn't worth much. But this is exactly backwards for public learning. The easier it is to share something, the more often you'll do it. The more often you do it, the more material you generate. The more material you generate, the more chances you have to discover what resonates, what connects, what's actually worth saying.

Ask yourself: what's the minimum viable version of sharing what I just learned? Usually it's one sentence. Sometimes it's a screenshot. Often it's a link with a single observation. Start there.

## Social media

Social media is maligned — often fairly — for being a waste of time, a vector for misinformation, a machine that rewards outrage over insight. All of that is true in certain contexts. But it's also the lowest-friction public publishing platform ever created. The barrier to entry is nonexistent. The distribution is built in. And for a public learner, the important thing is not the algorithm — it's the habit.

### The life-changing magic of the daily #TIL

"Today I Learned" posts are a perfect form factor for microlearning. They're short by convention, which removes the paralysis of trying to write something comprehensive. They're humble by nature — you're explicitly admitting you just learned this, which preempts any expectation of expertise. And they're useful by default, because if you learned something today, there's a reasonable chance someone else will learn it from you tomorrow.

Josh Branchaud has a GitHub repository where he's logged a TIL every day for years. It's one of the most quietly impressive knowledge artifacts on the internet. You don't need to do it daily. You don't need to do it on GitHub. But the daily TIL as a practice — noticing one thing you learned and writing it down publicly — is one of the most powerful habits a knowledge worker can develop.

### What if you don't know what you want to learn or write about?

This is the most common reason people don't start: they feel like they need a niche, a focus, a clear topic before they can begin. But the truth is that most people find their focus by learning in public, not before it. Your interests reveal themselves through the act of noticing what you keep coming back to, what you find yourself explaining to others, what rabbit holes you can't help going down. Pay attention to what you're already curious about. Start there.

## Writing stuff down and publishing it

There is a meaningful difference between writing something down privately and writing something down publicly. When you know someone might read it, you have to make it legible. You have to organize your thoughts. You have to confront whether you actually understand what you think you understand. Writing for a public audience, even if that audience is just one hypothetical stranger, is a forcing function for clarity.

### Commonplace books and resonance calendars

Commonplace books have existed for centuries — physical notebooks where readers copied out passages, observations, and ideas that resonated with them. Tiago Forte modernized this concept as the "resonance calendar": a running record of the ideas, images, quotes, and references that keep grabbing your attention. The commonplace book is not a summary of everything you've read; it's a curation of what stuck. That distinction matters enormously for a public learner, because what sticks to you is the raw material of your original thought.

### Arguments for plain text

Plain text is not glamorous. It's not a beautiful app with custom fonts and a dark mode that makes your notes feel like they were written in a cozy library. But it is permanent, portable, future-proof, and searchable. Files in proprietary formats get locked into apps that might not exist in ten years. Plain text files, especially Markdown, are readable by any text editor, any browser, any system, for as long as computers exist. For a public learner who intends to build up a body of work over years or decades, durability of format matters.

## Experimenting on different platforms

Not every platform will be the right home for your public learning. Different platforms have different cultures, different affordances, and different audiences. What works brilliantly on Mastodon might land completely flat on LinkedIn. What gets traction on YouTube might get no response on a personal blog. This is not a reason to avoid choosing; it's a reason to experiment without overly committing.

Try social media with short observations. Try microblogging platforms (Mastodon, Bluesky, Threads) for half-formed thoughts. Try GitHub for code, configs, and technical notes. Try a personal site or Obsidian Publish for curated notes you want to persist. Try writing code snippets and pushing them to a public repo. Try voice notes published as short audio clips. The goal at this tier is to find the form and platform that feels sustainable — not perfect, just sustainable — and start building the habit there.
